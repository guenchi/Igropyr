Reliability

Under load

Plateau

These tests were designed to probe the throughput ceiling of a single-core service under real network conditions. This did not find it. Two attempts hit two different walls, and neither of them was the server:

Driven from home fibreat around 16,000 small packets per second downstream the line's buffer gives out. The loss that follows corrupts the load generator's own accounting, so past that point the run stops being a measurement at all.
Driven from a second cloud hostsame private network, but the generator is single-threaded and never saturated the target: the figure rose every time another client process was added, which means what was being measured was the generator.

So the rungs below are a floor, not a ceiling. They do establish what a plateau looks like — and the rest of this page is not about peak throughput at all.

A single Igropyr process on a 2 vCPU / 2 GB Lightsail instance in Paris, one core, driven over a WireGuard tunnel from a European home broadband client.

The plateau itselfthroughput neither collapses nor oscillates as concurrency rises tenfold — it holds between 13,600 and 13,800, under 1.5% apart across four rungs. Concurrency past c=100 bought queue, not throughput.
The re-runc=1,000 was run twice in the same session — once before the host was pushed into its failure region at c=2,000, once after. The second pass reads 13,661 rps, p50 71 ms, p99 117 ms against the first pass's 13,652 / 71 / 112: the two rungs are all but identical — 0.07% apart on throughput, the same median to the millisecond, 5 ms apart at the 99th, zero failures both times. Being driven past its limit left nothing behind.
The 48 failuresthe client's, not the server's. Packet capture shows the server answering at its normal rate and then retransmitting each failed reply six or seven times; none of it arrived. The failure is in the network layer between them — the client received nothing to count. Igropyr reclaimed the stalled connections at the configured 30-second read timeout, exactly as specified. All 48 stalled at the same moment rather than scattered through the run — one event, not forty-eight.

* Why the ladder stops at 2,000

At c=2,000 the ladder pushes roughly 16,000 small packets per second down a domestic line and the burst overruns the home router's downstream buffer. The equipment in front of the server gives out before the server does, so the next rung up would be measuring the router.

Trying to break it

Asking a 512 MB host for 15,000 connections

This test probes how the server behaves under a sudden burst, and where its limit is. Three questions: under a burst, what throughput does it hold and how many requests fail; can it still re-form the mesh; and does paging brought on by the burst produce errors of its own.

So the run drags the server into swap from the outset: 15,000 concurrent connections on under 460 MB of available memory. As in the previous test, the node deliberately drops its link and redials every 20 seconds.

The service held 15,000 concurrent connections for about eight minutes. This is the throughput across those eight minutes:

What ran outnot the process — resident set holds 165–178 MB throughout. The kernel: sockets push wired pages to 203 MB, buffers another 50 MB. The Scheme heap was never the constraint.
Mesh, during the thrashingthe designed churn kept running: dropped and redialled every 20 s, 86 reconnections, 86 successes
Then the kernel killed itOOM, largest process. Up to that moment: zero server faults, zero failed responses, zero failed mesh re-formations. It was serving correctly when the OS took it away. The OOM killer fired twice over the run; the second time it took a sampling process.
After the killthe supervisor restarted it and three minutes later it carried the full load again — and was paging again.

What this test concludes

1. Cost per connection

per connection    9.8 KB  =  Scheme 6.9  +  kernel structures 2.2  +  in-flight mbuf 0.7
                 13.5 KB  =  peak, including memory not yet collected

fixed baseline    ~356 MB  =  kernel 230  +  Scheme baseline 106  +  userland 20

N  ≈  (total memory − 356 MB − reclaim margin) ÷ 9.8 KB

2. pageout I/O error started about three minutes before the kill, while resident set and free memory were both still wandering and saying nothing definite. That is the counter to alarm on.

So a 1 GB host can hold:

Per gigabyte, steady stateabout 30,000 connections with no paging at all. Not a projection: another test held at or above 30,000 through most of a 23-hour window on a host that size.
Per gigabyte, burstabout 50,000, riding the pager to do it — arithmetic, not measured.
The mesh

Kill a node, at random, a thousand times

This test asks how well the mesh rebuilds itself under extreme disruption and high latency.

Three nodes — Paris, Hong Kong and a Mac — over a cloud-to-cloud WireGuard tunnel. One of them was killed and restarted at random intervals for seventeen hours while Hong Kong carried 3,000 concurrent keep-alive connections.

Kills1,036 SIGKILLs of the Mac node, at random intervals — p10 34 s, p50 82 s, p90 133 s, max 149 s. Random matters: nothing in that spacing is a multiple of the 3-second reconnect base, so there is no phase lock to flatter the numbers.
Recoveries1,038 peer down against 1,040 peer up in the observer's log. A recovery that never arrived would show up as more downs than ups; none is missing.
Recovery time, the killed nodeN=1,037 — p50 4,803 ms, p90 9,126, p99 9,521, max 10,960. That includes the crash loop's own ~5 s restart before any dialling begins, so it is an upper bound on the framework's share, not the framework's share.
Deliberate link drops, Paris sideN=233, redialled immediately, p50 1,520 ms. The tail is not reported: p99 lands at 3,210 ms, which is the reconnect delay for attempt 0 (3 s ± 25%), so a few of them went through the timer rather than straight back — that tail describes the timer, not the recovery.
Accounting invariant, checked at rest57,062 checks, zero anomalies. 49 of them needed one more 250 ms poll before the system was quiescent enough to check.
Under load throughoutHong Kong was serving 3,000 concurrent keep-alive connections for most of the window — the kills were not done on an idle mesh.

What the soak asserts is the quiescent-state accounting invariant, not the ordering of down and up events — ordering is pinned by a single-process cell instead. There is no one-sided-reachability check in this run, so nothing here says the link was good in both directions at every moment.

What a single-process fixture adds

Replacement and identitya same-named node at a higher generation takes over, the old connection closes, the watcher sees node-down then node-up in that order; a monitor spanning the two incarnations answers oppositely for each, on purpose
Poison and late mailan event failing delivery three times is quarantined rather than dragging the node down, and can be listed and redelivered; a late mdown still reaches its watcher; dialling survives the registrar being killed
Close paths and the write gatesix TLS close paths — owner death, normal exit, double close, sealing, re-arm failure — each with its own cell; under the write gate two writers never interleave

These run in one process against a raw peer fixture that sends what a well-behaved node never would. Across real machines the equivalent adversarial cases are untested — the campaigns above interrupted the link; they never lied to it.
